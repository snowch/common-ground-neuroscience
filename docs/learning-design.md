# Learning design

Audience: interested adult beginners, choosing a short practical companion to the Common Ground courses. The gap is between a brain-related label and an accurate explanation that informs a feasible change and a meaningful test. The course teaches interpretation and planning; it cannot remove missing resources or diagnose another person.

Six café chapters connect neuroscience, cognitive psychology and behavioural evidence. Each has a first decision, three independently narrated explanations with a distinct figure, an evidence check, a new written transfer case, retrieval and a voluntary field application. A final five-part case integrates all six chapters. The intended outcome is a bounded explanation and justified action, not a brain score or a promise of optimisation.

The established Common Ground typography, pale blue surfaces, navy text and teal controls are intentional. Text, controls and figures stack in reading order on narrow screens; research is expandable and each visual teaches one comparison or process. Narration expands the visible concepts with reasons and examples rather than simply reading the points. Paragraphs have explicit pauses.

Page visits are not mastery; self-checks do not establish general competence. No independent academic review or learner pilot has been conducted.

## 1. A brain explanation should help us ask better questions

Objective: Given a claim about a person, distinguish neural evidence, psychological explanation and behavioural intervention, and identify what remains uncertain.

Initial decision: What should the team do first?

Transfer: A fictional workshop reports a brain-signal change after training but measures no change in work accuracy or daily functioning. What conclusion is supported by this result?

Application: Choose a public brain-related headline or a fictional claim. Write the actual observation, the proposed explanation and the practical recommendation separately. Name one missing comparison; do not label a real person from the headline.

## 2. Attention depends on the task and the conditions

Objective: Given a performance problem, distinguish selection, working-memory demands, switching costs and current state, then choose a relevant support and outcome.

Initial decision: Which first change best fits Jo’s account?

Transfer: A learner says they feel used to late nights, but makes more omissions on a comparable low-stakes practice task. Which interpretation is proportionate?

Application: Choose an ordinary task you already do. Identify its next relevant detail and leave a clear return marker before an optional interruption. Notice accuracy, effort and whether the arrangement fits your responsibilities. Do not restrict sleep or remove necessary contact to test the idea.

## 3. Learning changes systems through specific experience

Objective: Given a learning or brain-training claim, distinguish memory systems, task-specific plasticity and transfer, and design practice with a relevant check.

Initial decision: What should decide Sam’s practice first?

Transfer: A course advertises better everyday reasoning because participants improve on its memory game. What is needed to support the broader claim?

Application: Choose one small topic or skill you already want to learn. Attempt it before checking, use appropriate feedback and leave a question for a feasible later return. Include a new example at review. Judge the capability you practised rather than claiming a general brain upgrade.

## 4. Motivation is learning in a situation

Objective: Given a motivation or craving claim, distinguish reward prediction, cue-driven habits and practical opportunity, then choose a feasible change without inferring a neurotransmitter level.

Initial decision: Which question would most improve Leo’s explanation?

Transfer: A volunteer silences an optional alert during several feasible sessions and completes more planned paragraphs. The sessions were not randomly assigned. What does this show most directly?

Application: Choose one low-stakes action you want to make easier. Describe its cue, immediate consequence and a practical barrier. Prepare one voluntary change and a specific next step, then review what happened. Do not change medication or use a deprivation routine to test a neurotransmitter story.

## 5. Emotion and recovery belong to a context

Objective: Given an emotional or performance change, separate a bodily observation from an interpretation and compare practical, cognitive and recovery-related responses.

Initial decision: Which response best fits the café problem?

Transfer: A fictional workshop claims to remove chronic stress because participants report feeling calmer immediately afterwards. What should be checked before accepting the claim?

Application: Use a fictional ordinary frustration, or an example you find manageable. Separate the observation, interpretation and practical conditions. Propose a clarification, a change in the arrangement and a feasible recovery opportunity. Choose what fits; do not infer another person’s feelings from a device.

## 6. Step away, return and check the idea

Objective: Given a creativity claim, distinguish incubation, divergent generation and convergent checking, and design a bounded practical trial without treating neural activity as proof of benefit.

Initial decision: What would make the headline useful to test?

Transfer: A designer lists more possible layouts after a break, but has not checked whether any layout contains all required information. What conclusion fits the observations?

Application: Choose a low-stakes problem you already understand. Write what is needed and what you tried, take a feasible undemanding break if you want, then return to generate and check ideas. Record usefulness and cost, not a supposed brain score. A seated or otherwise accessible activity can be your practical option.
