c01s03 Chapter 1: One event can have several useful explanations.
Neuroscience asks how nervous-system processes support Sam’s performance. Cognitive psychology asks how Sam understood and remembered the instructions. Behavioural science asks which conditions influence the action and whether a proposed change helps. These questions can inform one another.

A neural account does not make the changed form irrelevant. Start with the missed details, ask Sam what happened, and compare possible explanations. Understanding a mechanism and establishing a useful intervention require different evidence.

c01s03b Chapter 1: A brain measurement has an interpretation.
A neural association links a measured signal with a condition or performance. It may help constrain an explanation without settling what caused the difference. Reverse inference goes in the other direction: from a brain observation to a claim about someone’s mental process.

That inference needs evidence about how specific the signal is, the comparison and the wider task. A region can participate in several processes. Lesion or controlled stimulation comparisons can help test causal roles, but the affected networks and task still need consideration. Seeing a signal associated with attention would not, by itself, establish why Sam missed a detail or reveal a stable personal weakness.

c01s04 Chapter 1: Useful change needs an outcome that matters.
A mechanism explains how something might happen. An intervention changes a condition, and an outcome states what would count as useful improvement. A plausible mechanism can guide a proposal; it cannot substitute for checking the outcome.

The café could compare checking accuracy with two equally accessible instruction layouts. It would also ask about effort and missed information. A different brain signal after a workshop would not establish that visitors receive more accurate bookings. Choose the practical question before choosing a measurement.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Neuroscience adds evidence about nervous-system processes and their constraints. Psychological studies and intervention comparisons answer related questions about thinking and action. Brain measurements can narrow explanations, but a correlation does not by itself identify a cause.

Reverse inference depends on the specificity of a signal and prior evidence; it is not automatically invalid or automatically justified. This fictional café case contains no measurements. A useful assessment would compare relevant conditions and meaningful outcomes, while asking the person rather than diagnosing from a distance.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Select the information the next action needs.
Attention prioritises information, while working memory helps maintain and use what the next action requires. Jo must keep a label detail available while following a checking rule. A task can become harder when several demands compete.

Distributed nervous-system processes support these functions; a single concentration score does not explain every lapse. A written checkpoint may reduce what has to be held in mind. To judge it, examine whether the relevant information is noticed and the check is completed accurately, not whether the person looks intensely focused.

c02s03b Chapter 2: Changing tasks can require changing the rule.
Task switching can require reactivating a rule, remembering where you stopped and inhibiting an earlier response. A switch cost is a measured performance difference in a defined comparison, not a universal number of lost minutes.

Jo’s interruption may create a return problem rather than a lack of motivation. A marker saying “label checked; confirm contact detail next” offers a concrete return. Necessary interruptions still matter. Compare accuracy, time and access before deciding which optional demands to change.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Feeling adapted is not a performance measure.
A state such as fatigue can vary across occasions. A subjective report tells us how the person feels; vigilance tasks measure a specified kind of sustained performance. These measures are related questions, not interchangeable ones.

In a sleep-restriction experiment, performance deficits accumulated while subjective sleepiness did not track them fully. That does not diagnose Jo from a single lapse. It suggests checking workload and recovery opportunities alongside interruptions, rather than treating confidence or one bad shift as a complete measure of capacity.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Task-switching studies compare defined tasks and conditions; they do not establish one fixed recovery time for every interruption. Sleep-restriction findings show that subjective experience and measured vigilance can diverge in studied conditions. Do not reduce sleep to recreate the experiment.

For ordinary improvement, choose a feasible support such as a clear checkpoint or fewer optional alerts, then judge the intended outcome and its costs. Neural control accounts explain possible constraints, while behavioural comparisons are still needed to establish whether the support helps in this setting.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Remembering is not one undivided ability.
Declarative memory supports reporting a fact or event. Skill learning concerns improvement in performing an activity through experience. The hippocampus and nearby structures are important for forming new declarative memories. Skill learning recruits other interacting systems, including circuits involving the striatum and cerebellum. These are useful distinctions, not perfectly isolated boxes.

Sam’s smooth routine does not establish that Sam can explain a new fault, and a correct explanation does not establish fluent tool use. State the capability you want: recall a principle, recognise a case or perform a sequence. Then choose a practice and a check that match it.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Plasticity is a capacity, not a guarantee.
Neuroplasticity describes changes in nervous-system structure or function. It does not specify whether a change is useful, how large it is or whether it transfers to another outcome. Transfer needs evidence beyond success on the trained task.

Training studies can relate practice to measured brain changes, while reviews of brain-training programmes question broad everyday claims. A colourful before-and-after image does not establish better judgement at the repair desk. Check the actual trained outcome and the untrained outcome separately, using an appropriate comparison.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Make the practice reveal the learning.
Retrieval practice asks Sam to produce an explanation before looking. Distributed practice separates attempts over time. Reviews of learning techniques support these practices across many studied conditions, but useful questions and intervals depend on the learning goal.

Sam could explain one fault, compare it with a trustworthy source and return later with an unfamiliar example. Feedback supplies corrections instead of rehearsing an unchecked answer. Neuroscience helps explain why learning has mechanisms and limits; the practical recommendation still needs evidence about what Sam can actually remember or do.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Memory-systems research draws on several kinds of evidence, including neurological cases, animal work and human experiments. No single system label describes everything a person can remember. Plasticity findings likewise need interpretation of the measure and task.

Evidence supports practice testing and distributed practice in many learning settings; it does not establish one timetable for every learner or topic. Brain-training reviews distinguish gains on trained tasks from broader transfer. For Sam, the meaningful check is a relevant explanation or repair outcome, not a brain score presented without that link.

c04s03 Chapter 4: A reward signal is not a motivation gauge.
Dopamine contributes to several functions. In reward-learning research, activity in studied dopamine pathways can track a reward prediction error: an outcome differs from what was expected. After learning, a cue can predict a reward, so the studied response is not simply a measure of how pleasant the reward feels. It is not a single pleasure or willpower level.

Leo’s phone checking does not tell us his dopamine level. The immediate expectation of news is one possible part of the learned pattern, but workload, the guide’s difficulty and access to tools may also matter. A mechanism should improve the questions we ask, not become a diagnosis from an ordinary action.

c04s03b Chapter 4: A cue can make a response more likely.
A habit is a learned response tendency in a familiar context. Outcome value can also change with the situation: an update may be interesting now and unimportant later. Habitual and goal-directed influences can coexist.

An alert, a familiar pause and an easy check may help explain Leo’s pattern. If the aim is a useful guide-writing session, one option is to change an optional alert or leave the next paragraph ready. Ask what checking provides before choosing an alternative. Removing a cue cannot supply the locked test device.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Choose the change by its effect and its costs.
An implementation intention links a situation to a response. Feasibility asks whether the action can happen under the actual conditions. Leo might plan to open the prepared paragraph after the desk closes, once the test device is available.

A small visible result may make the session easier to judge. Keep the goal personally meaningful and notice the burden as well as whether the action occurred. Neuroscience does not, by itself, select gradual reduction, swapping or stopping a particular cue. Those choices need attention to preferences, conditions and observed effects.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Reward-prediction findings concern specified tasks, pathways and comparisons. They do not let us infer a personal dopamine level from scrolling, a craving or a missed intention. Habits research likewise treats learned responses within a context, rather than equating every repeated action with an addiction.

Specific planning can support action in studied settings, but the proposed café arrangement is an application to evaluate. Keep the person’s goal and practical opportunity visible. A change in one routine does not establish a biological reset or identify a universally best method.

c05s03 Chapter 5: A bodily signal is one part of an account.

Jo’s pulse does not tell us whether the experience is fear, frustration, exertion or something else. Ask about the situation and Jo’s account. c05s03b Chapter 5: Change the demand when the demand is the problem.
A stress response involves coordinated brain and body processes, including autonomic activity and hormonal signalling. These can mobilise resources while also changing how attention and control operate. Its effects depend on the demand, duration, control and wider circumstances. Reappraisal changes an interpretation; it cannot supply missing staffing or resolve conflicting instructions by itself.

A manageable misunderstanding might be clarified by asking a question. A persistent queue problem may need clearer roles, capacity or access to breaks. Mechanistic stress research offers possible explanations and constraints, not a direct diagnosis of Jo or proof that one regulation strategy suits every situation.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Recovery is a condition, not a reward for output.
Circadian timing and sleep pressure contribute to when sleep is possible and needed. Memory consolidation concerns changes that stabilise or reorganise memories over time; sleep can contribute to learning and memory under studied conditions. These processes are not a promise that sleeping will solve every task.

For the café, the useful questions include workload, recovery opportunity and whether the next shift is feasible. Rest and enjoyable activity can matter in their own right. A personal routine cannot remove every institutional demand, and one wearable reading does not establish recovery or a sleep-stage diagnosis.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Emotion and stress involve interacting systems and contexts. Neural and physiological measures can inform an account, but one pulse value or brain-region label cannot establish a unique feeling, cause or suitable response. Some stress mechanisms are studied in animals or specialised human tasks; transfer to an everyday recommendation needs its own reasoning.

Sleep contributes to several functions, including memory, but a wearable’s estimate is not equivalent to a laboratory sleep-stage measurement. Keep practical constraints visible and do not deprive yourself of sleep to test this material. These lessons support interpretation, not diagnosis or treatment.

c06s03 Chapter 6: A break has a before and an after.
Incubation describes a pattern in problem-solving research: people return after a period away from a problem. Mind wandering is one possible feature of that interval, not a guarantee that useful work is occurring unconsciously. Effects depend on the problem and the intervening activity.

Leo first needs to understand the label problem. A feasible undemanding break can then be one option to try before returning. Missing information may instead call for investigation. The popular Jack Reacher label is the writer’s analogy, not an established neuroscience protocol or a reason to expect automatic solutions.

c06s03b Chapter 6: More ideas and a correct answer are different outcomes.
Divergent thinking concerns generating possibilities; convergent thinking concerns selecting or checking a suitable answer. A result on one kind of task does not automatically generalise to the other.

Walking experiments found benefits for idea generation under their study conditions, with a different pattern on a task requiring a single related answer. This does not establish a fixed percentage improvement for all problems. Leo can collect several label designs after a break, then check readability, missing details and accessibility. A fluent idea still needs evaluation.

c06s04 Chapter 6: A brain account still needs a behavioural test.
The default mode network is associated with several internally oriented processes. Executive control supports goal-directed responding. Creativity research examines interactions between networks; it does not describe a single creativity switch that turns on whenever attention turns off.

Resting activity is not proof that Leo’s particular problem is being solved. A useful trial would define the problem, the break or comparator, the return task and the outcome. Compare useful ideas and the quality of the checked result, while noticing time and burden. A personally helpful routine can remain a bounded finding.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Incubation and walking studies offer evidence about defined problems, intervals and outcomes. Undemanding activity has helped in some creative-task comparisons, particularly after initial exposure, but mind wandering is not a guaranteed cause of insight. Results do not mean that every break, every walk or doing nothing improves every problem.

Resting-state and network studies inform possible accounts of internally directed thought and control. They do not prove that a named popular method works. For an everyday trial, prepare, pause, return and check. For a strong causal claim, a suitable design and repeated, relevant evidence are needed beyond a few personal observations.
